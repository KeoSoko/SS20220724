I agree with the revised recommendation much more than the original metered-billing architecture.

The original design was technically elegant, but it turns Simple Slips into a billing platform:

Stored authorizations
Renewal jobs
Retry logic
Dunning
Seat snapshots
Charge reconciliation
Variable invoices

That's a lot of operational risk.

What I Think You Should Build
Phase 1 (Now)

Task #5: Workspace subscription inheritance.

This solves the immediate customer problem:

Company
├── Owner (payer)
├── User
├── User
└── User

Everyone can use the system.

One account owns billing.

Phase 2 (Immediately After)

Add Team Plans.

Plan	Users	Monthly
Solo	1	R49
Team S	2–5	R245
Team M	6–10	R490
Team L	11–20	R980

This is simple enough that:

Sales can explain it.
Customers understand it.
Support can troubleshoot it.
Why I Like This Better

Think about the three companies that approached you.

Nobody is asking:

"Can you calculate a dynamically metered monthly seat invoice based on active-user snapshots?"

They're asking:

"Can my team use Simple Slips under one company bill?"

The bucketed model answers that.

One Change I'd Make

I would not automatically upgrade people.

Example:

Team S
5 users

Then:

Owner invites user #6

Instead of silently charging more:

Show:

Your workspace has reached the Team S limit.

Current users: 5
Next plan: Team M
Monthly cost: R490

Upgrade required to invite additional users.

That prevents billing surprises.

What I'd Ask Replit Next

I would not ask for implementation yet.

I'd ask for a revised architecture focused on:

Team plans
Workspace billing ownership
Upgrade workflow
Invite limits
Paystack plan mapping
Migration from Solo → Team

No metered billing.

No authorization charging.

No renewal engine.

Just fixed Paystack plans.

That's the architecture I'd be comfortable building for Simple Slips today.

And importantly, if one day you reach hundreds of companies, you can still move to true per-seat billing later. Nothing about the tiered model prevents that future evolution.
